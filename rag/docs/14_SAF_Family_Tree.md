# SAF Family Tree

## Origins in Ancient Philosophy
The Self-Alignment Framework (SAF) is not an invention born in isolation. Its lineage stretches back over two thousand years to the thinkers of ancient Greece.  

Aristotle and Plato laid the foundation by asking the first systematic questions about the human mind. They observed the internal struggle between reason (what we know is right) and desire (what feels good). From this tension emerged the concepts of the Intellect (Reason) and the pursuit of a virtuous character. This was the earliest sketch of the framework’s family tree.

## Augustine and the Will
Several centuries later, St. Augustine expanded this two-part model of Intellect and Desire. He raised the central puzzle: how can someone know the right thing yet still choose the wrong one? His answer was the discovery of a third faculty—the Will.

The Will was the ultimate decider, the independent capacity to choose a path. With this addition, the family tree gained a crucial new member and a clearer picture of human agency.

## Aquinas: Values, and Conscience as an act
Two attributions belong to Aquinas, and one of them is usually missed.

**Values.** Aquinas uses *synderesis* for the habit that holds first principles without deliberating about them — the standard you do not re-argue every time you use it. That is exactly the role Values plays in SAF, and it is why the component that compiles the value set carries that name in the implementation. This is the oldest part of the framework.

**Conscience, but not as a faculty.** For Aquinas conscience is not a separate power at all; it is an *act* of the intellect — the one reasoning faculty applying moral knowledge to a particular case (Summa Theologiae I, q. 79, a. 13). The tradition treated it as a function, not a member of the family. SAF agrees in substance and departs in structure, instantiating that one faculty twice: once as the author of a draft, once as its independent auditor.

For the next 1,500 years this core was stable. Aquinas and later Kant debated the roles, but the membership did not change.

## SAF’s Rearrangement
SAF builds directly on this heritage but introduces a deliberate reordering. In SAF the sequence is:  

**Intellect → Will → Conscience**  

This differs from traditional accounts where conscience was more proactive, warning before action and accusing after. In SAF the Conscience is deliberately placed *after* the Will, as the judge of actions already proposed.

The cost of that should be stated plainly rather than softened. In a person, conscience is both proactive and reactive: it warns before an action and nags afterwards, and it operates through emotion rather than argument. Placing it after the Will **degraded that function** — it gives up the warning-beforehand half in exchange for a loop that is systematic and testable. A real loss, accepted deliberately.

## The Birth of the Spirit
Even with this arrangement, something was missing: the ability to capture long-term performance. Virtue ethics already holds that character is formed through habit, by repetition — *habitus*. What SAF adds is the **Spirit**: that idea given the role of integrator for the whole loop.

The Spirit tracks long-term coherence, measures how far a response sits from the character the agent has established, and holds the system's identity across turns. It is the only genuinely new member of the family.

Counting carefully: SAF has **five** faculties — Values, Intellect, Will, Conscience, Spirit. Four are inherited. The Spirit is the addition, and the ordering is the other one.

## Naming the Spirit
Choosing the term *Spirit* was not easy. The word is abstract and carries metaphysical weight, yet no other captures the concept as effectively. Spirit is the manifestation of alignment itself.  

When values and actions harmonize, a sense of meaning emerges. Scripture alludes to this in Luke 1:47: *“My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior.”* For me, this describes a spirit in perfect alignment.

## What the contribution actually is
Four of the five faculties come down from the tradition: two from Greece, the Will from Augustine, Values and Conscience from Aquinas. One faculty is new and one arrangement is new.

That is a smaller claim than "a new framework" and a more defensible one. If the parts have held up for two thousand years, the useful question is not whether they are correct but whether they can be made to operate — which is what SAFi is for.
